Satellite fire detections, weighted by wind direction, are commonly used in operational smoke
prediction but are rarely evaluated on their own merit. Using public METAR archives, we created a
dataset of twelve airports on Sumatra and Borneo where visibility was limited to less than 3\,km
due to hazy or smoky conditions (henceforth haze-limited visibility events). Then we investigated
whether including wind-directional features of satellite fire detections (from the VIIRS active
fire product) could improve 6 and 24 hour forecasts of haze-limited visibility events. To that end
we built an eleven-year dataset (2015--2025) and used leave-one-year-out cross-validation to assess
model skill. As baselines, we used: time-shifted data; randomising wind direction; categories of
wind reporting; a bootstrap method at regional and station-level; and a model without
wind-directional features (matched for missing data). The directional model did improve 24-hour
forecasts a little bit (\pv{loyo24.d.E-D0} average precision), but this was driven by a lot of
seed-to-seed variation in one extreme season, where different runs of the same model differ by
almost 0.1 average precision. When we did five-seed ensembles, the directional model was only a
few thousandths of average precision better than the permuted-wind placebo
(\pv{ens24.d.E-Rperm1}) and the controls, with no consistently larger effects with vegetation
fires only. The only noticeable benefit was that the fire count predictors helped 24-hour
forecasts a little bit. Overall, we recommend seed ensembles and mask-preserving placebos for
studies that claim directional wind-aware fire features are helpful.
